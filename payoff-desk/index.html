Strategic account / Sample Federal Credit Union (synthetic) / direct payoff

One credit union on Method: the go-live, then what comes next

An account plan in working form. Part one takes a credit union live on direct payoff for consolidation loans and proves each production behavior with a check that runs as a test. Part two sizes the next initiative on the loan book the first one creates. Code computes every figure on this page, and a second implementation in Python has to agree to the cent.

1The account

Who the customer is, what they bought, and what "live" has to mean before anyone calls it done.

Customer

Sample Federal Credit Union. It makes unsecured consolidation loans to members. Today it funds the loan to the member's share account and trusts the member to pay the cards off.

What they bought

Direct payoff. At funding, the credit union pays each creditor itself through Method, so the loan does what the member applied for.

What live means

Why it matters to the lender

A consolidation loan only lowers risk if the old balances actually go to zero. Direct payoff makes that a fact the lender can verify instead of a hope.

5What comes next

The first product creates a loan book. The next initiative is to watch it. Two uses of the same post-origination feed, sized on a synthetic book of consolidation loans. Change the assumptions and the numbers recompute.

The idea. After a consolidation loan, some members run their cards back up. The lender usually learns this when a payment is missed. Method's Portfolio Intelligence product keeps reading the member's accounts after origination, and Method's guide says its direct data shows a missed payment 30 to 45 days sooner than bureau reporting. That supports two actions: a second-look offer for members who can afford one, and an early call to members showing strain elsewhere.

What is mine here. Method's guide already names both uses. This section sizes them on one lender's book, says which to pilot first, and says why.

      7Coverage playbook, first draft

      How one strategic account could be covered in the first ninety days. Written to be argued with.

      Days 1 to 30: learn the account

      • Meet the five people in section 2. Write down each one's first question in their words.
      • Read the integration's exceptions queue for a month. It shows what the customer lives with.
      • Baseline three numbers with the customer: funded conversion, days to fund, exception rate.

      Days 31 to 60: bring two ideas

      • Bring two sized ideas in working form, like section 5, not as slides.
      • Let the customer break them. Record every objection as a requirement or a no.
      • Agree one pilot, its measure, and its stop rule before any build.

      Days 61 to 90: run one pilot

      • Pull in the forward deployed engineer when the pilot needs a change to the integration, and Sales when it needs a contract change.
      • Report weekly on the one measure. Stop on the stop rule.
      • Log every ask that a second customer would also make, and send it to Product as a proposal.

      8Platform notes

      Three things this build suggests for every integrating customer. These are a newcomer's notes from the public docs and repositories, offered as input.

      A go-live checklist page

      The docs cover each behavior on its own page. A single checklist a customer can run before launch would shorten the last week. Section 4 is a first draft.

      Webhook verification in the SDKs

      Signature and timestamp checks are documented, and customers write them by hand today. Community pull requests adding a helper were open on the SDK repositories as of mid November 2026.

      SDK parity with the spec

      The OpenAPI repository is public and current. A check that compares each SDK's resources to the spec on every release would catch drift before a customer does.

      How it works, and what it does not show

      The core is deterministic. The lending math is plain code: which accounts qualify, the quote, debt-to-income, the funding decision, and reconciliation. No model computes or writes any figure on this page.

      Two implementations must agree. The engine is JavaScript. A second implementation in Python, written from the policy text with decimal arithmetic, computes the same figures. The build fails if any figure differs. A separate script plants ten mistakes in the code, one at a time, and confirms the checks stop each one.

      What this does not show. It does not show behavior in Method's sandbox or production, where data and money are real. It says nothing about Method's internal systems. Sixteen checks are a starting list, not proof a real credit union is ready. The sizing in section 5 runs on an invented loan book.
